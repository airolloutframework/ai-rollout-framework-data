# Entity Definitions — AI Rollout Framework Glossary

Standardized definitions for the terms and concepts used consistently
across airolloutframework.com. These are the site's actual working
definitions, not generic dictionary entries — pulled from the live pages
where each term is used.

## AI readiness

How prepared an organization is to adopt AI responsibly and get real
value from it. It describes the conditions around the technology —
leadership clarity, governance, workflow fit, and team capability —
rather than the tools themselves. Most organizations that feel behind
on AI are not behind on technology; they are unstructured, which is a
different problem with a different fix.

In practice, AI readiness means an organization can answer four
questions without guessing: who owns AI decisions, what is and is not
allowed, which workflows are suitable candidates, and whether the team
has the skills to use AI well. An organization can be enthusiastic
about AI and still not be ready, because readiness is about structure
rather than interest.

Source: [airolloutframework.com/ai-readiness-score](https://airolloutframework.com/ai-readiness-score)

## AI readiness assessment

A structured way to measure how prepared an organization is to adopt
AI — scoring the conditions around it (leadership clarity, governance,
workflow fit, and team skills) rather than testing anyone's technical
knowledge. A useful one gives a baseline and a clear next step, not
just a number.

## AI Readiness Score

The free 16-question AI readiness assessment published by AI Rollout
Framework, covering the four capability pillars. Takes approximately
3–5 minutes; results are calculated instantly in the browser and placed
into one of three readiness stages: **Early Exploration**, **Developing
Capability**, or **Operational Readiness**.

## AI readiness framework

The model an organization uses to structure AI adoption — it defines
the dimensions readiness is measured on and the stages an organization
moves through as it matures. The framework is the map; an assessment
is how you locate yourself on it. The AI Capability Rollout Framework
is AI Rollout Framework's specific 90-day system built on this concept.

## AI readiness audit

A point-in-time review of where AI already stands in an organization —
which tools are in use, what data they touch, what guardrails exist,
and who owns the decisions — done before committing to a rollout. It
is diagnostic: the point is an honest picture of the current state, not
a plan yet. The free **5-Minute AI Audit** is a fast, five-question
version of this.

## AI maturity model vs. AI readiness assessment

The two overlap but answer different questions. An AI maturity model
describes the stages an organization moves through over time — commonly
awareness, experimentation, structured development, scaling, and
transformation — useful for tracking a multi-year trajectory. An AI
readiness assessment is a point-in-time measurement of whether the
conditions for a responsible next step are in place right now. Maturity
tells you how far along the journey you are; readiness tells you
whether you are prepared to take the next step.

## AI implementation roadmap

A sequenced plan that moves an organization from scattered, ungoverned
AI use to controlled, measurable capability — defining who owns the
rollout, what the guardrails are, which workflow gets piloted first,
and how progress is measured. Organized around decision gates, not
features: at each stage the organization either advances, revises, or
stops, based on documented evidence.

## Capability-first (vs. technology-first)

AI Rollout Framework's core positioning stance, and its signature line:
*"Responsible AI adoption starts with capability, not technology."*
Most published AI adoption guidance is technology-first — it opens with
tool selection, cloud infrastructure, or a data platform. Capability-
first reframes the starting point: an organization's AI capability is
built from leadership clarity, governance, workflow fit, and team skill
— the conditions a manager actually controls — not from which AI tool
is chosen. The technology is usually already present in the
organization; the missing piece is structure.

## Stage-gate / decision gate

A phase-boundary checkpoint in the framework's 90-day system. Each of
the three phases closes with a leadership decision gate: the
organization does not advance to the next phase until the prior phase's
work is documented and a written decision is made (advance, revise, or
stop). This is what distinguishes a structured rollout from open-ended
experimentation.

## Shadow AI

AI tool use by employees that has not been sanctioned, governed, or
made visible to leadership. Employees adopt consumer AI tools because
they make work easier, often before any policy exists — banning it
tends to drive usage underground rather than stopping it. Covered in
depth at [airolloutframework.com/resources/employees-using-ai-without-approval/](https://airolloutframework.com/resources/employees-using-ai-without-approval/).
